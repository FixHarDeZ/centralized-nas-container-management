# Product-image prep — turning Fix's screenshots into a clean ingredient

Fix's screenshot is always the primary ingredient (the Shopee listing itself is WAF-blocked), and he usually sends the SHOP'S AD GRAPHIC: a composed promo image with the product plus Thai promo copy, discount/coupon banners, a CTA button, a shop logo and sometimes a Yen/Baht figure on a card preview. Sometimes it is instead a plain product PHOTO pasted inside a listing frame — the promo lives in the frame's white margin, not the photo — and that shape has its own fast locator (step 2a). The no-price HARD RULE applies to the PIXELS you attach, not only to the voiceover — but the answer is to salvage the image, not to skip it, because a clip with no product shot is worse than no clip.

**Decision before any salvage — vision-read the source once, then branch.** ONE read on the FULL image asking for every readable text plus any price / currency / discount / coupon / QR / shop wordmark. Any of those present → run the salvage recipe below. NONE present → attach the ORIGINAL file un-cropped: the no-price rule bans price / promo / wordmark TEXT, not ad layout, and cropping a clean ad only throws away product context (branding, spec badges, feature icons) the model can use as reference. Name the branch in the delivery — which file was attached, and that the source was checked clean.

## Time budget — 2 vision checks per reference (Fix 2026-11-12)
A vision call takes 20–150 s here. One job spent 55 min on ~14 crop → check rounds for a single reference
(anttermite, 2026-11-12). So:
- Make ALL candidate crops first (different sources / margins), paste them on ONE sheet with a big label per
  tile (PIL, labels from code), and ask vision once: which tiles are free of text / price / UI / people and
  show the whole product.
- Use pixel probes (recipe below), not vision, to find edges and to confirm a fix.
- A second sheet is allowed after fixing what the first check named. No third: send Fix the best candidate
  with the remaining defect in one line and ask `clarify` (use it / he sends another photo). Zero spend while waiting.
  (The zoom reads in "Review-screenshot salvage" count against this budget.)

## Lifestyle photos — a styled room is a valid reference
Shop photos often show the product set up in a real room: a clothes rack holding clothes and a bag, a shelf with jars, a desk with a lamp. When the photo has no text/UI/people, it passes the photo gate AS IS. Do not refuse it, do not ask Fix for a plain-background photo, and do not try to cut the product out (thin white frames on a pale wall do not survive a cutout, and the props show what the product is for). (2026-11-10: a clothes rack in a bedroom was refused as "not product-only" and stalled the order.)
- The ~1000 px floor in `house-rules.md` is for ad screenshots where the product is a small part of the frame. A lifestyle photo where the product fills most of the frame is fine at Shopee's usual ~750–800 px.
- Attach the whole photo. Crop only when a test shot shows the model merging an edge object (bed, vase) into the product.
- In the clip prompt, name the product precisely ("the white clothes rack with a wheeled basket") and list the props as allowed set dressing that is NOT part of the product ("clothes, bag and baskets are styling, not included"). This replaces the single-product stray-prop line in `templates/clip-prompt.md` for this case.
- The risk is the structure warping, not the background: keep the product static, use a slow push-in or pan, and have the presenter stand beside it and point. No rolling the product and no handling the items on it.
- Narration and pills talk about the product only. Never imply the props come with it.

## Use-context gate for review and listing screenshots
A crop from a buyer's review photo or a listing thumbnail often shows the product as shipped: loose parts, a tray on cardboard, the item unassembled beside its screws. That crop is not a valid shelf, rack or appliance reference, because the model then invents the installed form. Before offering any such crop as the reference, vision-read it once and require that the product appears assembled and in its use context (on or beside the appliance it is meant for). When only loose-part crops exist, say so in the question to Fix, with the options (wait for a clean installed photo, or accept the structure risk), and spend no credits until he picks.

### Review-screenshot salvage — strip the UI band first
A review post (buyer photo + comment UI) is often the only photo of the exact product Fix has. Salvage it in two stages:
1. **Cut the screenshot's UI out before touching the product.** Ask ONE layout read with bounds as FRACTIONS of the image (0.0–1.0), never pixel coordinates: vision's pixel answers come back in whatever canvas it assumed and two reads disagree by tens of px, while fractions are scale-free and self-consistent. Crop the PHOTO band out first, so the @handle, star row, price bar and avatar are outside your working rect from then on and can no longer ride into any later crop.
2. **Adjudicate hands and cut edges with targeted zoom reads, not hue probes.** On warm-lit photos every skin predicate also matches the beige background and the product's own orange label art, so a skin-count probe finds "fingers" on label graphics and misses pale skin. Settle "is a hand in the crop / is the base cut" with ONE zoom read of that zone (tile several suspect zones into one sheet if there are several), and take the product's top/bottom from row profiles instead: count near-white pixels per row across the product's x-range — the first and last row with a real count are the nozzle top and the base bottom. Vision's completeness verdicts flip between runs; the row count does not.
3. A sliver of finger at one corner is Fix's explicit call, not yours: offer it alongside the small-source options in the same clarify, then record the accepted risk in the storyboard (drift check before the next shot's submit) and in the delivery.

## Salvage recipe — locate, cut, verify by pixel probes
1. Copy the cache image into a scratch dir under a descriptive filename and `ffprobe` it for WxH (the picker lists by filename, so make it descriptive).
2. **Locate the product, trying in this order — stop at the first that returns numbers:**
   a. **Frame scan — run this FIRST whenever the screenshot is a product PHOTO pasted into an ad frame** (a Shopee/Amazon listing shot: photo rectangle surrounded by a white margin that carries the price tag, the QR code and any promo strip). Run it only on that shape: on a COMPOSED ad (product on a gradient with copy flanking it) every column comes back non-white, the "largest run" spans the whole canvas and the result means *skip to (b)* — it is never a crop. The ad elements then live entirely OUTSIDE the photo, so you do not need the product's bbox at all — you need the PHOTO's. Scan for pure white (`r>248 and g>248 and b>246`) per row and per column, take the largest contiguous non-white run on each axis, and crop to that rectangle: one cut removes price + QR together. Verify by an edge test, not vision: count white pixels along all four crop edges and require 0 — a nonzero bottom edge means you are still inside the frame's margin (the frame can end mid-row, so re-scan the exact boundary row rather than nudging by eye). Deterministic, no vision round-trip, and it beats asking where the product is when the whole problem is where the FRAME is.
   a2. **Brightness-step profile — the first choice whenever the product is ONE solid object sitting on a busy, textured or DIAGONAL-WAVE background** (no white frame to scan for, and the product's hue is shared with the ad art so 2c's mask covers the canvas). No colour predicate at all: sample the MEAN brightness of every column over a y-slice safely inside the product's vertical span (`colmean(x, y0, y1)`), then the mean of every row over an x-slice inside its horizontal span. Background columns/rows read a steady plateau; the product's first column/row steps by hundreds. Print the profile every ~4 px to find the band, then re-scan that band at 1 px for the edge. This found all four edges of a game case on a red/white diagonal-wave ad in one pass with zero vision round-trips — it needs no reference column, no hue and no prior bbox, which is why it beats 2c (hue-dependent) and the per-row reference sampling in the gradient section (which needs a known-empty column).
   - **Sanity-check the result against the product's known proportions before cropping.** A retail case, box or book has a stable aspect ratio (a Switch case is nominally ~0.61 w/h, but an ad-composited or perspective render measures 0.58–0.61). Divide measured width by measured height — anything in 0.55–0.62 is independent confirmation that all four edges were found, and only a way-off ratio means one edge is still background (re-scan that axis rather than trusting the jump you saw). Do not re-scan a box that already passed the edge-touch test just because it lands a few percent under the nominal ratio.
   - **A narrow strip whose brightness is CONSTANT across every height while the background beyond it changes is the product's drop shadow, not the product.** Sample the strip at 2–3 y positions: a real part of the object co-varies with the scene lighting and with the object's own edge; a shadow reads the same ~10-value band top to bottom with whatever background lies behind it (white at the top of the ad, dark at the bottom). Crop flush at the object's own edge and exclude the strip — including it puts a dark bar down one side of every frame the clip renders from it. Conversely, when the object's own bottom rim differs from the background but the background row-matches across the full canvas width, that rim IS the object: end the crop on it.
   b. ONE `vision_analyze` on the FULL original asking for `left_x, top_y, right_x, bottom_y` of just the product, with the image's WxH stated in the question. **Stating the size does NOT pin the canvas — the model answers in a size it assumes (it reported 1000×999 for a 950×950 ad even with the size in the prompt), so divide every returned coordinate by `assumed_width / ffprobe_width` before cutting; an unscaled bbox is off by ~5 % and clips the product's edge.** This used to work reliably; the model now OFTEN ignores the format and answers with a prose description of the ad instead. If you get prose rather than four integers, do not re-ask — go to (c). **A MAP question elicits locations better than a BBOX question does:** asking it to map EVERY element (product, every readable text with its approximate location, QR code, shop wordmark, regional badge) returns a located table even when "give me four integers" comes back as prose — and the product's bbox then falls out by difference, because the price / QR / logo zones it names are exactly the zones your crop must exclude. Feed that table into (e)'s profile refinement; never treat it as a measured edge.
   - **Even after the ratio-scaling above, treat the bbox as approximate by a few–tens of px per edge and pad the cut OUTWARD when the ad elements sit far from the product.** Vision placed a box's bottom edge ~50 px above its real one on a 950² ad even with its own dimension claim accounted for, so a cut flush to the estimate clips the very edges the four-edges gate then rejects. With ±15–20 px of margin outside the estimate there is nothing for the extra band to leak in, and the existing strict four-edges verification converges in one round — no pixel probe needed. Reserve flush/tight cuts for ads whose promo elements hug the product, where (e)'s profile is still the measured edge.
   c. **Hue mask in PIL** — mask pixels of the product's own colour and take the bbox of that mask, but ONLY when that hue is absent from the ad's art: a bright-yellow vehicle on a red/white ad masks cleanly, while a red box on a red/coupon ad (see Dead ends) masks the whole canvas. Count masked pixels per row and per column, then take the contiguous band holding ≥ ~12 % of the peak on each axis (thin same-hue strays — text strokes, logos — fall outside the band) and join gaps ≤ 3 px. **Pad that band out hard**: the mask covers only the coloured bodywork, so the real product (black wheels, seat, mirrors) is far larger — expect to roughly double the band's span before it contains the whole object.
   d. Otherwise crop by position (hero product off-centre, promo copy left, CTA strip bottom) and let the verification below converge.
   e. **Refine the resulting box with column/row profiles before trusting it — this applies to a vision bbox too, even when vision answered with four integers.** A guessed bbox is not a measured edge. For x across each of its side margins, over the box's y-range, count dark pixels (`max(rgb)<90`) and ad-red pixels (`r>170, g<70, b<70`) per column: background columns read ~0 dark while the product's first column jumps by hundreds; the same profile run per row finds the top and bottom. Crop just INSIDE the jump — losing a few px of product beats letting a sliver of ad margin ride along the edge (a red stripe 5 px wide on the left edge is the ad's banner still in frame, and only this profile shows it). One pass converts a plausible bbox into a clean one; no vision round-trip needed.
3. **Verify with pixel probes first; treat vision prose as corroboration.** `vision_analyze` frequently ignores "answer tersely / binary only" and returns a full description, so do not spend rounds forcing a yes/no — measure instead, in pure PIL (about a second on a 950² image):
   - *Edge-touch test*: count product-hue and dark pixels touching each of the four frame edges. A nonzero count on an edge means the product is cut there (e.g. right edge 207 px → the tail ran out of frame); top/bottom/left at 0 means those sides are clear. Extend the cut on any offending side and re-measure. A couple of pixels where the tyre meets the frame bottom is acceptable when the body is fully inside. **A crop cut exactly ON the product's own boundary legitimately shows dark on all four edges** (a flat-sided product fills its own bbox), so dark alone cannot separate "cut" from "flush". Disambiguate with the AD's colours: count banner-red and pure-white pixels in the outermost columns and rows — a nonzero count USUALLY means the margin is still inside the crop, and the fix is to move that edge inward a few px, not to re-derive the whole bbox. **On a RED-background ad this test cannot reach zero at a correct cut: the product edge anti-aliases into the background red, red bleeds through that blend zone, and the product's own art can carry the same hue — a residual count of a few hundred px along a ~700-row edge is FLUSH, not margin. There take the edge from the VALUE TRANSITION instead (scan each side column across the product's rows for where the steady background breaks into artwork), confirm with the 0.55–0.62 aspect check, and stop nudging inward: crops that keep 'failing' the red count while the aspect passes mean the count is wrong, not the crop (same move as the gradient section's per-row sampling — decide by value change, not by colour membership).**
   - *Promo-band map*: count pure promo-colour pixels (the ad's banner red: `r>170, g<70, b<70`) in ~25 px bands across the region you are about to keep. Wherever they start, your bottom edge is inside the promo strip — move the bottom ABOVE that band, then re-run the edge-touch test (the product's own last row is found by scanning row-runs: the product is one wide contiguous run per row, promo glyphs are scattered short runs on different rows).
   - *Stray ad text inside the crop*: scan for glyph-like pixel runs in the kept region (non-background density maps — an 8×8 grid of non-background counts, then a fine ASCII map over any hot cell). A single line of small dark glyphs at a corner is ad copy (variant colour labels, wordmarks), not the product.
4. **Removing a stray glyph line without damaging the product or the background:** paint the text's bounding rect (a few px of margin) by vertical interpolation — for each column, blend from a clean row a few px above the rect to a clean row a few px below. Guard the rect first with two tests: (a) NO product-hue pixels inside it, and (b) dark pixels are confined to the text's own y-band — glyph antialias cores ARE dark (`min(rgb) < 90`), so a bare "dark pixels present" must NOT abort the paint, only dark pixels outside the text band must. Keep the rect off background boundaries too: a red/white diagonal edge crossing the rect gets smeared by the interpolation, which is visible damage — start the rect just right of such an edge instead of covering it.
5. Ship the clean crop and tell Fix in the delivery which promo elements you cut away (headline, 0 % / installment banner, bank logos, CTA button, variant-colour label).
   - **Run the final listing read as FIVE questions on EVERY crop, in one call:** every readable text verbatim, any price/currency, any shop wordmark, whether any HUMAN OR HUMANOID FIGURE appears, and WHAT PRODUCT the crop shows — a real photo, a face at any size, or a rendered/stylized game character printed on the product's own artwork (character livery on a controller strap or faceplate counts). The figure question is not optional — Flow's prominent-person refusal is driven by the ATTACHED image, not the wording, so a figure caught here costs one vision round-trip while one that reaches the submit costs a whole refused generation (SKILL.md → Pitfalls). **A read that answers "no real human face" while character art sits ON the product is NOT a clearance**: ask about humanoid figures explicitly, and when the product's surface is covered in character art, crop a region WITHOUT a figure (grip, panel, spec band) instead of shipping the whole product shot. Ask it of the crops, not only of the source ad: a rectangle placed by eye can put a head at an edge the full-image read never mentioned.
   - **A reference that shows a real child or toddler is not attachable, even when the product is clean.** Ad composites often put a child in the bath or in use beside the product. Drop that image, or crop to the bottle only with the child fully outside the crop, and verify with a humanoid read on the crop. Vision bboxes around such ads place the bottle's base close to the child, so pad outward and verify.
- **A crop whose identity reads as a DIFFERENT product than the one being sold must not be attached — and that check runs on YOUR crops, not only on photos Fix sends.** Re-read it once: if the two reads disagree about what the object even is, the identity is illegible, and an illegible reference cannot be one — the model draws whatever it guesses, so the wrong subject reaches frame. Drop that item from the reference set, name the drop and the reason in the delivery, and never voice over an item you could not attach.
   - **An OPTIONAL extra that cannot be cropped complete is dropped after one measurement pass, not approximated.** Bundle contents laid out on the ad's own background hue with a neighbour tucked under one edge have no measurable edge on that side, so every crop either includes the neighbour or clips the item — and a clipped reference makes the model invent the missing part. A coarse colour-classified ASCII map of the region (PIL, ~a second) shows in one look which sides are unmeasurable; when it does, cut your losses, drop the item and spend the remaining round-trips on the hero product's crop instead.
   - **Judge a brand fragment sliced by your own crop edge against the SOURCE ad, never against a single read.** Vision names a different company on each pass over a logo cut mid-word, so 'shop wordmark found' from one read is not proof — compare with the original, and keep such a fragment out of frame by moving the crop edge rather than by trusting either reading.

## The ad background is often a GRADIENT, not white
The frame scan (2a) and the edge test (3) both assume pure-white margins. A common variant puts the product on a light-blue / lavender GRADIENT panel — store wordmark at one corner, a regional flag badge at another — with a separate white band along the bottom carrying the price and the QR code. The white scan then finds no frame at all and every column reads "product", which looks like a composed ad and sends you to a bbox hunt you do not need. Define background as a PREDICATE, not a colour: `r>245 and g>245 and b>245` (white) OR `b>=g>=r and b>200 and r>140 and g>160` (a light-blue gradient) — row/column profiles over `not isbg(...)` then land the product's left and right edges as a jump in the count, exactly as step 2e describes. Two probes finish the box:
- **Bottom edge = where the gradient meets the white band.** Scan down a column inside the product until the pixels turn pure white, then step back to the last non-white row — the frame band is a hard boundary, so this is a measured edge, not an eyeballed one.
- **The ad side panel is also "not background",** so a horizontal profile PLATEAUS at the panel rather than dropping to zero. Take the product's right edge from the last big jump in the count, not from where the counts reach zero — the gap between those two x values is the width of the ad panel you would otherwise leave in the crop.
- **When NO colour predicate covers the background (a dark→bright red gradient, for one), sample it per row instead of classifying it.** A red mask returns the whole canvas here (see Dead ends), so pick a column you know is empty background — between the product and the side panel — and, for each row, compare every pixel against `px[ref_x, y]`, counting the columns whose max channel delta exceeds ~45; the product's edge is where that count jumps. The reference is taken on the SAME row as the test, so it works on any gradient without needing to know what colour the background is.
Verify the final crop with the edge-touch test as usual, and specifically re-check the two bottom corners: the price band and the QR code live in that white strip and will ride along if your bottom edge sits even a few rows low. Then run the listing-text verification (one vision read listing every readable text) — a clean crop answers with the product's own branding only (PS5 bar, game title), which is what confirms no promo copy survived.

## Composite ads — several products, copy scattered everywhere
A multi-item promo graphic (2–3 same-hue items staggered across the frame, Thai promo copy above them, a cashback badge at one side, a trust strip along the bottom) defeats every bbox locator: the items touch, merge into ONE connected blob and their bounding boxes overlap. Map the canvas first, then choose crop RECTANGLES — never try to segment objects.
- **Layout grid first (~25 px cells):** paint an ASCII map classifying each cell by priority — product hue (A) > promo red (R) > near-black glyphs (D) > white (.) > other — printed with row labels. One screen shows every copy zone and product cluster at a glance, no vision round-trip, and it beats a single bbox hunt for this shape.
- **On a FLAT saturated background (solid red, solid blue), skip hue-mask tuning and use a DOMINANCE predicate instead.** Background pixels are strongly single-hue, so "is this pixel dominantly the background colour" — e.g. `r - (g+b)/2 < 60` for red — separates content from backdrop in one pure-PIL pass with no reference column, no per-row sampling and no mask threshold to calibrate. Scan ROWS over a column-slice inside the product band to get each object's top/bottom, then COLUMNS over that row-band for its left/right; the START/END transitions are the measured edges. This is the cheapest locator for the layout where several SAME-COLOUR products stack vertically down one side while a scatter of same-hue small parts sits on the other: it yields each object's extents independently, so every subject gets its own tight crop instead of one rectangle spanning the whole canvas. Verify each crop with the four-question listing read (step 5), NOT with the 0.55–0.62 box-ratio check — a handheld console with grips and a cluster of loose button caps have no fixed retail-box aspect.
- **Two reads of the SAME crop disagreeing about which border clips the product is a tie, not a defect signal — resolve it by pixels, never by a third read.** Vision answers border yes/no questions inconsistently on busy crops (one read "all four clear", the next "left and right clipped", on identical input). Re-derive the extents with the dominance/row-column scan above and trust those numbers; do not keep re-reading hoping for agreement, and do not widen a crop the pixel scan already shows has margin.
- **Exact ad-element rects from connected components** of the promo-red and dark masks (walk with ~2 px dilation, keep components ≥ ~150 px): banner strips, price figures, cashback badge, CTA button each come back as its own rect — those are the zones your crops must MISS.
- **Touching/overlapping products cannot be cropped per object:** component-splitting slices the neighbours. Pick 2–3 OVERLAPPING rectangles that together cover the whole cluster while clearing every ad-element rect; overlap between your own crops is fine (they are references, not a jigsaw). A promo prop planted ON an item (a red flag tag stuck to a bottle) is cleared the same way — move a rectangle edge past the prop's rect or drop that item's crop; never inpaint over busy product art.
- **When the objects OVERLAP each other AND share the ad's background hue, they are not croppable at all — drop them rather than attaching a partial.** Neither edge is then measurable (the neighbour hides one side, the hue hides the other), so no rectangle passes the four-edges gate, and a crop that fails it hands the model a missing side to invent. Judge that from one colour-classified ASCII map of the region, not from repeated vision reads: the reads disagree with each other about which border clips, which is the tie already resolved by pixels, not a cue to re-read.
- **Same hue ≠ ad copy:** thin red marks staggered identically on every item can be each item's own logo, not promo — colour alone decides nothing. Run the listing-text verification from the watermark section (crop the element's own region small, ONE vision read listing every readable text): product branding printed on the item → keep it; promo wording → the rectangle must clear it. When two reads disagree on the wording, the wording doesn't matter — a coloured prop bearing script is already ad copy.

## Watermarks and annotation lines drawn ON the product
Ads frequently stamp a SECOND shop's watermark over the product artwork itself (a small logo badge in one corner, a store name running vertically up an edge, a barcode graphic) and draw measurement annotation (a red dimension bracket labelled "80 CM" / "30 CM") ACROSS the product rather than outside it. The recipe above assumes ad elements sit in the margin; these do not, so they need their own handling.
- **Do NOT paint a watermark out of detailed artwork.** The vertical-interpolation removal in step 4 is for a stray glyph line over a FLAT background or a smooth product surface. Over busy art (foliage, neon, texture) it leaves a hard-edged rectangular smear that reads as image corruption — worse than the watermark it removed. Paint only where the surrounding pixels are near-uniform; everywhere else, crop.
- **Crop to an INTERIOR rectangle as the default remedy.** Cut a rect strictly inside the product that clears every watermark corner, sacrificing product area instead of damaging art. A wide flat product (desk mat, mouse pad) tolerates this well — a ~500×160 interior strip still reads unmistakably as the product and still fills a 9:16 frame.
- **Mask annotation colour per-REGION, never globally.** A red dimension bracket is easy to find with `r>170, g<70, b<70`, but the product's own art may be full of the same hue (red tropical plants in a jungle scene), so a global red mask returns the whole canvas and a red-based crop destroys the product. Locate where the red count is locally high per row/column, crop INSIDE the annotation, and leave same-hue pixels that belong to the art.
- **Per-row extent scanning finds the true product edge under a skewed shot.** For a wide product photographed in perspective, print `(min, max, count)` of non-annotation pixels per row across the band: the extents reveal the perspective slant and exactly where the annotation stops overlapping, so the crop boundary is measured rather than eyeballed.
- **Vision's watermark bboxes are unreliable — verify by LISTING text, never yes/no.** Two reads of one crop returned different shop names AND different coordinates, so use pixel probes to choose the crop, then run ONE `vision_analyze` asking it to list EVERY piece of readable text. A clean crop answers with the product's own branding only — a brand wordmark printed ON the product (e.g. "LEGION" on a mat) is the product, not a watermark, and must stay.

## Watermark sitting in the BACKGROUND beside the product
A clean product shot — a case or box on a plain white margin — often carries the shop's wordmark in a corner OUTSIDE the product, touching nothing but background. There is nothing to inpaint, and an interior crop needlessly sacrifices product area; what you need is the exact column where the product ends and the wordmark begins.
- **When no crop can clear the mark, fill its rect with the SAMPLED background colour.** That happens whenever the mark's rect falls INSIDE the product's bounding band — it shares rows (or columns) with the product, so cutting to the product's edge leaves the mark in frame and cutting past it slices the product (two cases side by side spanning the same rows as a corner stamp is the classic case). Sample the background from a clean cell instead of hard-coding white, overwrite the rect with a few px of margin, and let the fill be invisible — which is the same condition that governs painting anywhere in this file: only over near-uniform surroundings. The "do NOT paint out of detailed artwork" rule is unchanged; this is its flat-background branch, chosen because cropping is unavailable.
- **Derive that rect from a pixel scan, never from vision's bbox, and re-scan after filling.** Vision put a corner stamp's top edge ~50 px below its real one, so a fill built from the quoted bbox leaves a residual sliver of glyphs that the closing listing read reports as "a tiny stray mark". Count non-background pixels per row and per column over the corner region to get the true extents, fill, then require ZERO non-background pixels inside the filled rect before any vision check; residuals cluster just ABOVE the fill line, so extend the rect upward and re-scan rather than calling the first pass clean.
- **Print dark runs per row across the disputed band.** For ~8 rows spanning the watermark's y-range, list every contiguous run of `min(rgb)<200` from the product's edge out to the canvas edge: the run that reappears at the same x on EVERY row is the product (or its shadow); runs that shift, split or appear only on some rows are glyphs. Crop at the last product run — one cut keeps the whole product and drops the wordmark.
- **Bound the watermark's zone with a hue scan scoped to the background first.** Blue wordmark text on white finds itself with `b>110 and b>r+45 and b>g+25`, but only when the scan runs OUTSIDE the product estimate — the same test over the whole canvas returns the product's own art and a bbox spanning both, which looks measured and is useless for cropping.
- **Never take the crop edge from a connected-component bbox in this geometry.** Specular glare on shrink-wrap SPLITS the product so the component stops short of its true edge, and a wordmark touching the product MERGES into it so the component runs on into the glyphs — both failures return a plausible rectangle. Per-row run extents are the measured edge; the component bbox is not.
- **A grid overlay does not upgrade vision to a measuring tool.** Drawing 100 px gridlines with coordinate labels and asking vision to read them off beats prose but still lands tens of pixels off on BOTH edges (product edge pushed out, wordmark pulled inward), so use it only to decide which side the wordmark is on, then measure with the run scan.
- Close with ONE listing read: a clean crop answers with the product's own branding only.

## A QR code PRINTED ON the product is packaging, not an ad element
A Switch 2 game-key-card case carries a printed QR on its own white key-card strip, and a shop's promo QR sits in the ad's margin. Only the second one has to leave the crop — a QR inside the product's own boundary is part of the object the clip is meant to show, so keep it and do not report it as a leftover in the delivery. Distinguish them by where they sit, not by what they look like: if the code lies inside the bbox you measured for the product, it is packaging; if it lies in the margin band you cut away, it was the shop's. Asking vision whether "a QR is present" alone cannot tell them apart and will make you cut product area for nothing.

## Watermarks banded across the top/bottom margins — crop the BAND, not a bbox
When every ad element sits in a horizontal margin (shop wordmark top-right, search-bar logo bottom-left, platform badge bottom-right) while the product(s) occupy the middle of the canvas, you need no element rectangle and no product bbox — only where the content band starts and ends, and one full-width cut drops every mark at once. Measure the band in one PIL pass: print the ROW white-fraction every ~15 px (rows at ~1.0 = empty top margin, falling to ~0 = content) together with per-row horizontal edge energy (peaks mark the product's top/bottom borders), take the first and last content rows, then `ffmpeg -vf crop=W:H:X:0`. This is the cheapest locator for a canvas holding TWO products side by side, where the 0.55–0.62 aspect sanity check cannot apply — a band wider than one box is not a failed edge detection, so judge that crop by the listing read instead of the ratio. If the map-style `vision_analyze` of step 2b answers with prose instead of coordinates (it does so often on this shape), do not re-ask: run the band probe instead, then close with the four-question listing read (step 5).

## Two product photos side by side, split by a white gutter — crop ONE panel
An ad that pairs two shots of the same product (a stretch/durability demo beside an installed-use shot) with a plain white column between them needs no bbox: scan every column over the content rows for a full-height pure-white run (`r>248 and g>248 and b>246` on every sampled row) — that run is the gutter, and each side is already a complete panel. Crop whichever side matches what the clip needs, then close with the four-question listing read (step 5). It beats a bbox hunt because the panels' own edges are measured by the gutter rather than estimated, and it lets you DROP a panel outright instead of trying to crop its contents apart — e.g. keep the cat-in-window shot and discard the demo shot carrying a human hand, which is the cheapest way to keep a person out of the attached set (SKILL.md → Pitfalls, prominent-people refusal). The top/bottom ad copy still spans the whole canvas, so run the BAND rule above for the rows and this gutter rule for the columns: two independent axes, one cut each.

## When every ad crop is unusable — official brand renders (Shopify storefront JSON)
Salvage has a ceiling: an ad whose product surface is covered in character/humanoid art, or an ad showing a DIFFERENT product that rode along in the same listing's gallery, cannot be cropped into a safe reference no matter where the rectangle is placed — and re-cropping the same ad costs vision rounds while changing nothing. The Shopee listing itself stays WAF-blocked, so go to the BRAND instead:
- Most accessory brands run Shopify. `curl -s 'https://<brand-domain>/collections/<handle>/products.json'` returns the whole collection with `title`, `handle` and `images[].src` (Shopify CDN URLs — no cookies, no WAF, no JS). Discover the handle by curling a collection JSON (e.g. `/collections/new-arrivals/products.json`) and matching the product TITLE — never by scraping the storefront HTML page, which renders client-side and comes back with no product links at all.
- Official renders are usually clean of price / discount / QR / shop wordmark, but they are NOT pre-cleared: a themed-edition render carries the same character livery that trips the prominent-person refusal (SKILL.md → Pitfalls). Download several, tile them into ONE contact sheet, take a single humanoid/text read over the sheet, then the four-question listing read (step 5) on each crop you actually attach.
- Pick shots by what the CLIP is about: a "new colourway" clip needs the colour plus the theme lettering, and a rear/side render is often the clean one because character art lives on the FRONT face. 1–2 renders beat a pile — every attach is a picker round-trip.
- Attach the ORIGINAL file (PNG uploads fine in the picker); do not re-encode to save bytes. State in the delivery that the clip shows the brand's official render rather than the shop's ad composite.
- **Console storefronts publish the same data as JSON inside their own pages and do NOT WAF like Shopee.** `curl -sL -A 'Mozilla/5.0' https://www.nintendo.com/us/store/products/<slug>/` returns 200 with the product's `productImage` CloudinaryAsset embedded in the page HTML (a `"publicId":"store/software/<platform>/<productId>/<40-hex>"` pair, plus a `productImage({"shape":"square"})` variant that IS the cover art). Build the reference big enough yourself by inserting a Cloudinary transform — `https://assets.nintendo.com/image/upload/w_1600/q_auto/f_auto/<publicId>` — because the JSON's own `url` is `q_auto` only; that transform is what turns a store listing into a ≥1000 px clean cover reference in one request.
- **TRAP: one page's HTML contains assets belonging to OTHER products** (merchandising banners, related-title cards sit beside the real one), so grabbing any image hash you spot yields a different game entirely. Take the hash ONLY from the `productImage` entry whose `<productId>` matches THIS product's own id, and answer step 5's "what product does this show" question on the download before it becomes a reference.
- **Official publisher sites crawl to nothing useful at hero size.** `<publisher>/games/<title>` HTML yields logo, bonus-card and screenshot paths at ~300–640 px — good for small post-production stickers (the bonus cards a physical edition ships with, a game logo), too small to anchor product identity as `ref1`. Harvest them for `graphics_layer` image stickers, not for the reference.
## Small-source crops — product under ~1000 px in the ad
When the product covers only ~300 px of a ~950 px source (a composed ad with the product beside a face), the crop cannot meet the 1000 px reference gate. This is an accepted-risk path, not a silent fallback:
1. Ask Fix once with `clarify`, giving the three options (send a clean ≥1000 px product photo / use the small crop with the quality risk / hold the order) and a numbered text list in the chat, because an unanswered question must cost zero.
2. Locate the product with one vision bbox read (ratio-scale it to ffprobe W×H), pad 15–20 px outward, then cut with PIL. Check the cut face is product-only (no face, hand or price band inside).
3. Upscale the crop so its longest side is ~800 px with LANCZOS. Upscaling adds no detail; it only meets the picker's size expectations and keeps the attach loop unchanged.
4. Drop any source whose crop still contains a face or hand, and record the drop with its reason. A face-bearing frame is never salvaged by padding.
5. Name in the delivery that the references are small crops and that the product surface may read soft on screen.

## Tool facts for this host
- **Bottle-on-ad crops from a vision bbox: pad the BASE outward.** The bottle's base is the edge vision clips most often (measured on a 950 px ad: the base sat on the crop's bottom edge). Extend the bottom by 15–25 px past the vision estimate, then ask one vision read whether the base is cut; a cut base also means the cutout reads as a different product.
- `ffmpeg` and `ffprobe` are available. **`PIL` may be missing from BOTH the PATH `python3` and the `execute_code` kernel** — probe once (`python3 -c 'import PIL'`, and inside `execute_code` if that fails), and when neither interpreter has it run the probe FILE with `uv run --with pillow python <probe.py>`: `uv` is installed on this box and pulls Pillow into an ephemeral env, so nothing is installed on the host and the probe file itself does not change. **`numpy` is NOT installed anywhere** — don't plan a numpy pipeline, and don't `import numpy` and burn a call discovering it.
- **Write multi-line PIL probes to a scratch `.py` file and run the file — never `python3 -c "..."`.** Nested quotes die in the shell, and a `%` anywhere in the same command string (a curl `-w 'HTTP %{http_code}'`, a regex) makes Python raise `ValueError: unsupported format character` before any of it runs, which reads as a broken probe rather than a quoting bug. The file also survives the session, so a re-run with one changed constant costs no re-typing.
- **A single-box ad needs no PIL at all — this is the FAST PATH:** one product (a game case, a box) sitting on a gradient panel with a corner shop wordmark, a regional flag chip and a bottom promo/search bar, no price printed anywhere. `ffprobe` the source for WxH → ONE map-style `vision_analyze` (recipe 2b) to place the ad elements and the product → cut with `ffmpeg -vf crop=W:H:X:Y` (offsets from that map, padded inward a few px) → ONE vision read listing every readable text to confirm only the product's own branding survived. Skip the pixel probes unless that read names a leftover ad element — they earn their round-trip on white/gradient FRAME ads and on composite clusters, not on a box isolated in the middle of a canvas.
- A pure-PIL pixel loop over a ~950x950 ad runs in about a second if you genuinely need one.
- **Locating a solid coloured callout box beside a product:** scan each row from the right half for pixels of the callout's hue and take the minimum x over the rows where it appears — that minimum is its left edge, and cropping just left of it excludes the callout. Vision's coordinates for the same box were off by tens of px, so use the scan.

## Texture-only crops are material references, not product references
When the largest text-free rectangle of an ad keeps the fabric or body but drops the feature that identifies the product (a valve, a label, a logo), the crop is only a MATERIAL reference. Two rules follow:
- Name the missing identifying feature in the Flow prompt explicitly (shape, colour, position) and say in the delivery that the reference shows material only, so the model is not free to invent the object.
- If the crop also cuts off the identifying feature, ask Fix for a clean full-product photo once (with the cost in the question) before spending credits; a crop that shows the material but not the product is weaker than a prompt-only description.

## When the text-free area is too small to show the product, stop cropping
Ad layouts put headlines and badges across the product area. When the remaining clean rectangle no longer shows the whole product (the handle is cut at the top, the mop head is missing, the valve is out of frame), another salvage pass costs vision rounds and still leaks text. Ask Fix for one clean product-only photo instead (the order waits, nothing is spent).

## Dead ends (do not re-walk)
- **Hue segmentation fails only when the hue is shared with the ad's art** — mask the strong-red pixels of a red/coupon ad and the mask covers the whole canvas (background art, banners and prop graphics are the same hue), so the bbox comes back as the full image; crop that one by POSITION — but position need not be a guess, because a same-hue product still separates by **run structure**: per row, take contiguous runs of the shared hue and look for the band where ONE wide run spans the product (a case's solid colour top bar gives exactly that) while the ad's own rows fragment into scattered runs; confirm the sides with a dark-pixel column profile over the product's artwork and the top/bottom with a per-row background-white fraction (rows at ~1.0 = banner strip, rows near 0 = product band). One PIL pass, measured edges, no vision round-trip. When the product's hue is absent from the ad (yellow vehicle on red/white), the same mask is the fastest locator there is (recipe step 2c). Decide by hue, not by rule — and for a photo-in-a-frame screenshot, step 2a beats both, since it locates the frame instead of the product.
- **Asking vision for a pixel bounding box ON A CROP.** Pointed at a 460 px-wide crop it returned coordinates in the ORIGINAL 950 px image's space (`right_x: 764` > the crop width), i.e. it answers about the product, not about your crop frame. Never interpolate a bbox from a crop-space answer.
- **Chasing a formatted answer out of vision.** The same model that ignores "output exactly four integers" also ignores "answer in under 30 words" and "binary only" — repeated rephrasings buy prose, not compliance. Ask for the full-image bbox ONCE; if it comes back as description, switch to the pixel probes (recipe step 3) rather than spending more calls on wording. A prose read that happens to mention no promo text and no cut-off parts is a useful second opinion, never the primary check.
- **Treating a printed set/model number as legible.** Repeated vision reads of the same box returned OP-15, OP-06 and OP-16 in turn. When two reads disagree (or the code is small/stylised), the code is illegible — keep it out of the clip entirely (see the variant-mismatch rule in SKILL.md step 1) rather than trusting one reading.

## When the listing and the art disagree
The listing Fix pastes and the ad he attaches can name different variants (link says one set number, the ad banner says another). Do not silently pick one: keep the variant code out of both voiceover and captions so the clip cannot contradict the box on screen, then say so plainly in the delivery and offer to re-shoot the clip from a correct photo — that spends another clip of credits, so it is his call, never a silent re-run.
